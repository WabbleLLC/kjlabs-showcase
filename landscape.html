Briefing · 03

Where the demand is, and what people use instead

A public view of the ground this work stands on: where the facilities are, what they use today, and which business a two-person engineering team can actually start. Figures we could not read at the source are marked rather than estimated.

01

Where the demand is concentrated

The Midwest is not a marketing choice. It is where the facilities, the practitioners and the field data are.

The installed base

Grain storage is concentrated

Per USDA NASS, the U.S. had 7,844 off-farm grain storage facilities with 11.9 billion bushels of capacity (Dec. 1, 2025). Kansas holds 685 of those facilities and 1.22 billion bushels — third in the country behind Illinois and Iowa. Ten Midwest states account for 4,618 of the facilities and 8.09 billion bushels. Kansas is third in off-farm capacity but only thirteenth on-farm, so its storage is overwhelmingly commercial and professionally operated.

The hazard

The failures are recorded

In 2024 there were 9 U.S. grain dust explosions with 2 injuries and no deaths, per Purdue University. In 2025 the U.S. still had 7 grain dust explosions, killing 4 people and injuring 10 (Purdue University), including one each in Minnesota and Nebraska; Purdue's own list adds North Dakota and records three of those four deaths at a single biofuel plant in Nebraska on 29 July 2025. Purdue's ten-year table gives 85 explosions, 16 dead and 83 injured over the decade to 2025, and Kansas alone has recorded 60 since 1958 — five of them in the last ten years. The seven incidents of 2025 ran across two grain elevators, a farm-operated elevator, a seed plant, a feed mill, a flour mill and a biofuel plant, with three of the ignition sources never identified. Not one industry, and not only grain elevators: dust collection in these plants is a safety system, not a convenience.

The consequence

Penalties are published

OSHA 29 CFR 1910.272 sets the federal baseline for grain handling, mixing measurable thresholds with procedural duties. A repeat or wilful violation carries a maximum of $165,514 after OSHA's 2026 penalty adjustment, per the OSHA penalty memo. State air, fire and licensure rules sit on top of the federal baseline and change from state to state.

And what a plant actually uses

Mostly it is not other software. It is the spreadsheet, the vendor's own sizing sheet, and the consulting engineer who has seen it before.

What people use todayWhere it worksWhere it breaks
Spreadsheets and rules of thumb Fast for a single duct, familiar, free, and the person who built it understands it. The trade press says plainly that custom spreadsheets built on industry standards beat generic software for this work. Fitting losses live in cells whose source is a memory. Nothing checks transport velocity across a whole system, and nobody else can reproduce the calculation a year later.
Equipment manufacturers' selection software Authoritative for that manufacturer's own catalog, and free to the specifier. Tied to one vendor's products and published curves. It sizes equipment, not the system around it, and it is not a compliance record.
General-purpose CFD and FEA packages Capable of far more than this problem needs, and trusted by reviewers when used correctly. Expensive, expert-operated, slow to a first answer, and disconnected from the compliance question. The engineer builds the case, runs it and interprets it every time.
Simulation and design consultants Deep expertise on demand, with no tooling to maintain. Cost and turnaround per question, and the knowledge leaves with the engagement instead of staying with the plant.
Combustible-dust consultants and hazard-analysis providers The recognized route to a defensible dust hazard analysis and its paperwork. No provider we found publishes a price, so every engagement starts with a quote. Essential for the safety case, but they are not sizing the ductwork in the same pass, so the calculation and the evidence stay in separate documents.
Generic documentation and knowledge tools Good at storing files, tickets and pages. They do not understand a duct, a fitting loss or a cited requirement, so the engineering context has to be rebuilt by hand.
03

Why the gap is real, in three sentences

A result without a source is not usable

Every number the tools produce carries the table, log or requirement it came from. Without that, an engineer cannot defend the answer in a review, so the answer gets redone by hand.

Saying "not computable" is the feature

Most tools return a number whether or not they have the inputs for it. This system returns a third outcome and names the missing input, which is what makes it safe near a compliance decision.

The record is what compounds

A layout, a solve and a requirement check are worth little if the next engineer cannot reopen them and see why the answer was what it was.

Two honest limits on that claim. In our search we found no product that stores grain-handling or combustible-dust requirements as machine-checkable, per-jurisdiction records — that is a statement about our search, not a proof that nothing exists. And the regulator's own rulebook is not machine-readable: OSHA publishes its combustible-dust enforcement directive as a PDF only, per the directive page. Where the rules are not readable by a machine, a person has to re-read them every time.

No model is chosen on this page. Which one comes first is exactly the question an advisor is useful for. Two of these can produce revenue inside a quarter — engineering services, and a tightly scoped compliance gap assessment against a named requirement set — because both sell work a facility already budgets for. A per-seat product business is not a first-quarter revenue play.

05

Midwest, then national, then beyond

Expansion in this field is not a marketing decision. Each jurisdiction is a separate body of work.

Midwest first

The facilities, the universities, the extension networks and the founders' own field experience are in the same region. Pilots are reachable by car, which matters more than it sounds when the work involves walking a plant and measuring a real duct run.

United States second

The federal baseline is shared, but state air, fire and licensure rules are not. National reach means encoding rules jurisdiction by jurisdiction, and it means the structural and airflow capabilities have to hold up outside grain milling: feed mills, ethanol plants, food processing and any bulk-material handling that generates combustible dust. The ethanol sector alone runs 191 plants in the United States, 177 of them in the central region that includes Kansas and Nebraska, per the U.S. Energy Information Administration.

Outside the United States, later

Other jurisdictions are their own body of work, and Europe shows why: the EU regulates explosion-risk equipment and workplaces through two separate directives, applied across the member states' own national systems. Canada mixes federal chemical-hazard rules with provincial occupational safety law, and Australia has its own standard alongside state-adopted model law — all of it planned and not built, and none of it in code. In each case the rule text, the evidence expected and the language all differ. We treat international work as planned and not built: no jurisdiction outside the United States is encoded or claimed today, and expansion would be a deliberate program rather than a translation exercise.

What we have not established

  • No software market size. The market reports we found cover hardware and simulation services, not this. We have not read one at the source, so we quote none.
  • No hazard-analysis pricing. Not one provider in this field publishes a price; every engagement starts with a quote.
  • No consulting rate anchor. We have not verified what U.S. engineering consulting charges for this work.
  • No willingness-to-pay evidence. Nobody has been asked to pay yet.
  • Our own validation number is not independently corroborated. The closest published analogue we could find claims only agreement within its experimental uncertainty, so expect a technical buyer to probe ours.
  • No knowledge-loss figure. The widely quoted percentage traces to an infographic whose method we could not read. We will not repeat it.
  • Solver licensing. Some open-source solvers we use are copyleft, which may constrain how a closed product can be distributed. That needs counsel, not our own opinion.

Where this page states a number it names the source in the same sentence. Where the evidence was not available, the page says so instead of estimating — an advisor can help fill a gap, but cannot fix a number that was invented.
